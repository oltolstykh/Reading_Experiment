The limits of rational choice

For most of the twentieth century, economic theory was built on a simple idea: people are . According to this view, consumers and managers collect all the relevant information, compare the costs and benefits of each option and then choose the one that serves their interests best. This model made economic behaviour easy to describe with mathematics, and it is still useful for many purposes. However, anyone who has ever bought something they did not need, or delayed saving for retirement, knows that real decisions rarely look like this.

Shortcuts and biases

Behavioural economics emerged when researchers began to combine economics with psychology. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people often rely on , mental shortcuts that allow them to decide quickly without analysing every detail. Heuristics save time and effort, but they also lead to predictable errors. One of the best-known examples is : losing 100 euros feels considerably worse than gaining 100 euros feels good. As a result, people may refuse a fair bet or keep a failing investment simply because they do not want to accept a loss.

3

What is a nudge?

In 2008 the economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein published the book Nudge, which turned these findings into a practical approach. They argued that every decision takes place in a particular context, and the way this context is organised, the , inevitably influences what people choose. A nudge is a small change in this architecture that makes a desirable choice easier or more attractive. Importantly, a nudge does not forbid any option and does not significantly change financial incentives. Placing fruit at eye level in a canteen is a nudge; banning sweets is not.

The power of defaults

The most powerful nudges usually involve the , the outcome that occurs if a person does nothing. Pension schemes provide a famous example. When employees must actively sign up for a company pension, many of them never do so, even if they intend to. When they are enrolled automatically and are free to , participation rises sharply (Figure 1). The financial conditions remain exactly the same; only the starting point has changed. Several countries, including the United Kingdom, have used this insight to increase retirement saving.

Nudges in business

Companies have also learned to apply behavioural insights. The way information is presented, or , affects how customers perceive a product: shoppers tend to rate minced meat labelled “75% lean” more positively than meat labelled “25% fat”, although the two descriptions are identical. Similar techniques work in other areas. Energy companies, for example, have reduced household consumption by sending customers reports that compare their energy use with that of their neighbours. Such social comparisons change behaviour at a very low cost.

Ethical questions

Critics, however, point out that the line between helping and manipulating is thin. If a company deliberately makes it difficult to cancel a subscription, it creates what Thaler calls , friction that discourages people from acting in their own interests. For this reason, many researchers argue that nudges should be transparent and easy to avoid, and that their goals should be ones that the people affected would accept. Used responsibly, nudges remain a cheap and effective tool; used carelessly, they can damage the trust on which markets depend.
