Nudges: How Small Changes Shape Economic Decisions

For most of the twentieth century, economic theory was built on a simple idea: people are rational agents. According to this view, consumers and managers collect all the relevant information, compare the costs and benefits of each option and then choose the one that serves their interests best. This model made economic behaviour easy to describe with mathematics, and it is still useful for many purposes. However, anyone who has ever bought something they did not need, or delayed saving for retirement, knows that real decisions rarely look like this.

Behavioural economics emerged when researchers began to combine economics with psychology. The psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky showed that people often rely on heuristics, mental shortcuts that allow them to decide quickly without analysing every detail. Heuristics save time and effort, but they also lead to predictable errors. One of the best-known examples is loss aversion: losing 100 euros feels considerably worse than gaining 100 euros feels good. As a result, people may refuse a fair bet or keep a failing investment simply because they do not want to accept a loss.

In 2008 the economist Richard Thaler and the legal scholar Cass Sunstein published the book Nudge, which turned these findings into a practical approach. They argued that every decision takes place in a particular context, and the way this context is organised, the choice architecture, inevitably influences what people choose. A nudge is a small change in this architecture that makes a desirable choice easier or more attractive. Importantly, a nudge does not forbid any option and does not significantly change financial incentives. Placing fruit at eye level in a canteen is a nudge; banning sweets is not.

The most powerful nudges usually involve the default option, the outcome that occurs if a person does nothing. Pension schemes provide a famous example. When employees must actively sign up for a company pension, many of them never do so, even if they intend to. When they are enrolled automatically and are free to opt out, participation rises sharply (Figure 1). The financial conditions remain exactly the same; only the starting point has changed. Several countries, including the United Kingdom, have used this insight to increase retirement saving.

Companies have also learned to apply behavioural insights. The way information is presented, or framing, affects how customers perceive a product: shoppers tend to rate minced meat labelled “75% lean” more positively than meat labelled “25% fat”, although the two descriptions are identical. Similar techniques work in other areas. Energy companies, for example, have reduced household consumption by sending customers reports that compare their energy use with that of their neighbours. Such social comparisons change behaviour at a very low cost.

Critics, however, point out that the line between helping and manipulating is thin. If a company deliberately makes it difficult to cancel a subscription, it creates what Thaler calls sludge, friction that discourages people from acting in their own interests. For this reason, many researchers argue that nudges should be transparent and easy to avoid, and that their goals should be ones that the people affected would accept. Used responsibly, nudges remain a cheap and effective tool; used carelessly, they can damage the trust on which markets depend.

Glossary

choice architecture
the way the situation in which people make a choice is designed and organised (архитектура выбора)
default option
what happens automatically if a person does not make an active choice (вариант по умолчанию)
framing
presenting the same information in different words so that it is perceived differently (эффект формулировки)
heuristics
simple mental rules that help people make decisions quickly (эвристики)
loss aversion
the tendency to feel losses more strongly than gains of the same size (неприятие потерь)
opt out
to choose to leave a scheme or system that you were included in automatically (отказаться от участия)
rational agents
people who always use all available information and logic to choose the option that is best for them (рациональные агенты)
sludge
unnecessary obstacles that make it harder for people to do what is good for them (искусственные препятствия)
