A new way of working

1

Not long ago, a typical career meant a permanent contract, a regular salary and one employer for many years. Today a growing number of people earn money through short-term tasks, or “gigs”, which they find through a . Taxi drivers who accept rides through an app, couriers who deliver meals and freelance designers who complete projects for clients around the world are all part of what is called the gig economy. For many of them, a smartphone has replaced the traditional office.

How platforms make money

2

The business model of a platform is based on matching supply and demand. The company owns neither cars nor restaurants; it owns software that connects customers with workers and takes a on each transaction (Figure 1). Because the people who do the work are usually treated as rather than employees, the platform does not pay for their equipment, insurance or holidays. This keeps fixed costs low and gives the business high : it can expand into a new city quickly without building offices or hiring large numbers of staff.

Freedom and flexibility

3

Supporters of the gig economy emphasise its advantages for workers. People can choose when and how much they work, which suits students, parents and those who want to combine several jobs. Entry barriers are low, since many platforms require little more than a phone and an identity document. For companies, the model offers the ability to respond to changes in demand almost instantly, for example by attracting more drivers during busy evening hours through higher prices.

Many platforms require little more than a phone and an identity document.

The hidden costs

4

Critics, on the other hand, argue that this flexibility has a price. Earnings depend on the number of orders, so gig workers often face and find it hard to plan their budgets. They usually have no sick pay and no employer contributions to their pensions. In addition, their work is organised by : an app assigns tasks, sets prices and evaluates performance through customer ratings. A worker whose rating falls too low may face , which means losing access to the platform, often without a clear explanation.

The question of employment status

5

The central legal issue is . If gig workers are independent contractors, they are responsible for their own social protection; if they are employees, the platform must provide it. Courts and governments have reached different conclusions. In 2021 the UK Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers should be classified as workers entitled to the minimum wage and paid holidays. In 2024 the European Union adopted a directive that introduces a legal presumption of employment when a platform controls how the work is performed.

Looking ahead

6

For managers, the debate is not only legal but also strategic. A business model that depends on low labour costs may become less profitable if regulation changes, and the public increasingly expects companies to treat people fairly. At the same time, many workers value the independence that platforms offer and do not want to lose it. The challenge for the coming years is to find forms of work that combine the flexibility of the gig economy with the security of traditional employment.
